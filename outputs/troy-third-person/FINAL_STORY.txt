The Fire Beneath the Bronze

The surf had beaten gray kelp and broken pine ribs into the gravel south of the Myrmidon shelters, where charcoal trenches smoldered under damp sand. Thestor worked before the wind died. His nephew, Meles, sat on an overturned trough with their possessions drawn together in a goat-hair blanket—two spare tunics, an oilskin pouch of salt, and the small rasps his father had brought from Euboea when the fleet first gathered at Aulis.

Two bronze braces for a steersman’s box lay across Thestor’s anvil stone. He had driven new rivet holes through both, clearing the burnt wood of the old rudder post with a square reamer. Across the shingle, Ithacan rowers dragged casks of pickled tunny toward a beached merchant coaster whose master had already agreed to take wounded spearmen back across the Aegean. Thestor needed a place upon that deck for the boy before the autumn gales shut the northern passages entirely. The boy’s skin had turned the yellow of dry tallow after three months of camp fever; he had no strength left to haul ash oars or feed charcoal to the furnace.

At dawn, while dragging drifted timber from the surf, Thestor’s boot had struck something heavy in the weed. It was neither a ship’s bolt nor an anchor stock, but a hollow bronze sleeve as long as an arm, thick-walled and cold, choked with hardened brine. One end was raggedly torn, turned outward like the rim of a broken cauldron where fire had burst through an unvented mold. It bore no eye for a rope and no flange for a wooden shaft. Scraping the sea salt from the thicker base with a horn wedge, Thestor had uncovered two impressed stamps near the seam: a mallet crossed with fire tongs, and beside them a zigzag line broken by three hammered dots.

His father had shown him that same double mark once on the rim of a votive basin stolen long ago from the sea shrine below Cape Lekton, an inlet where the southern currents tore at the Troad’s rocky foot. Smiths in that sanctuary did not cast bronze for camp trade; they served an older altar hidden beneath the limestone shelves. When the horn scraper bared the tongs and the hammer, Thestor had laid his coarse greasy wiping cloth back over the metal and tucked the sleeve beneath his leather apron. To show unusual bronze openly in this camp was to invite the heralds of the Mycenaean king, who seized every pound of copper for the long-shield men and paid in promises scratched upon clay.

Odysseus, son of Laertes, came down the shingle with two spearmen trailing at his back. He had no armor on, only a dun cloak pinned at the shoulder with a boar’s tooth, but his gait was rapid and measured. He carried the master’s cedar tally stick, checking each cask as the crew rolled it over the skids.

Thestor did not wait for the prince to reach the water’s edge. He carried the two finished steering braces out to the skids and set them before Odysseus’s feet. He asked for the coaster’s third thwart: passage across the sea for Meles, together with a half-measure of barley from the royal stores, in payment for refitting the rudder box and replacing the pintles on two light cutters.

The Ithacan glanced at the work. He tapped the bronze with the cedar tally, testing the ring of the metal, and shook his head. The steersman’s box was sound, but three slingers blinded by dust at the Scamander ditches had already been brought down from the ditch lines; the master had no thwart left for an unseasoned boy who could not work an oar. The King of Mycenae had also ordered all shared grain gathered for the wall guard, since the Myrmidons still refused to haul flour from the magazines.

Thestor stood his ground between the prince and the sea. If Meles stayed through another moon among the rotting ditch trenches, the camp sickness would finish him before the fleet saw another spring. He drew the heavy cylinder from beneath the apron, letting the stained cloth drop into the shingle. He did not hold it out as a gift; he held it across his two forearms like an iron pig offered for weight, letting the morning sun strike the crossed tongs and the three broken dots.

Odysseus set both hands upon the metal. His hands did not hurry, but his fingers traced the thick, seamless wall and dipped into the torn hollow at the fractured end, feeling the strange scorch within. He turned the piece until the stamps caught the light. He knew the Lekton sanctuary. Twelve seasons past, on his first voyage toward the Hellespont, his steersman had run an ox-carrying barge onto the reefs below that cape; the priests there had demanded a heifer and three silver rings before they would show the sheltered cove behind the headland where fresh springs ran through the rocks. The prince rubbed the mallet mark with his thumb, then turned the broken end toward the sea. If the storm had torn such bronze from the sanctuary, the little cove might now admit men where its gates had kept them out. He offered a sack of summer barley from his own hut and an order for the boy’s passage on the next ship outward, provided Thestor went south with him in an eight-oared skiff to identify the smith-marks on whatever bronze lay scattered beneath the Lekton cliffs.

Thestor looked past the Ithacan to where Meles sat clutching the blanket. A journey south along the Trojan shore brought its own spear-risk, and the sanctuary lay close beneath the wooded spurs where the Dardanians kept watch fires. Yet the boy’s mouth was cracked with thirst, and the camp had no grain to spare for smiths without royal patrons.

He agreed to the oars and the journey, but would not release the broken cylinder until two of the Ithacan spearmen carried the barley sack across the gravel and placed it beside Meles’s trough. Odysseus watched the grain delivered without complaint. He then gave the tally stick to his steersman and motioned the master of the coaster to take aboard an archer whose shoulder had been smashed by a chariot wheel.

As the rowers shoved the merchant ship off the stones, its hull groaning over the wet rollers, Thestor walked back to his hearth. He quenched his charcoal with sea water, packed his copper hammers and the square reamer into his leather apron, and slung the heavy sack of tools over his neck. Meles was already chewing a handful of the raw, hard grain. Thestor took the boy by the shoulder, led him to the shelter of the Ithacan hut where the barley could be guarded, and turned his back on the open water to follow Odysseus’s skiff south toward the rocks of Lekton.

* * *

At Cape Lekton the ebb had left the skiff upon grey silt. Odysseus and his oarsmen hauled it above the wash before they began the climb. Thestor carried the broken sleeve under his cloak. Where the path narrowed, he passed it up to the man ahead of him and followed on hands and knees. A wave struck the rock below; spray swept the ledge, and one of the rowers lost a sandal. They waited while he bound his bare foot in a strip cut from a sail. By the time they reached the sanctuary, the leather around the bronze had soaked through.

The entrance lay behind a fallen lintel. Within, a hearth burned beneath a roof darkened by generations of smoke. Its keeper sat beside an anvil, turning his white-clouded eyes toward the scrape of their feet. He was an old man, with cords on his wrist to which small bronze tokens had been tied. Thestor laid the fragment before him. The keeper found the broken edge by touch and fitted his thumb into the hollow.

He required the return of the basin taken from this shrine: broad-lipped bronze, with crossed tools and three broken strokes stamped below its handles. Odysseus knew where it stood. The attendants of Agamemnon poured water into it before the king ate, and emptied it upon the ground when his hands were clean.

The basin had come into the camp among other plunder. Odysseus had been present when the shares were laid out. He had let the vessel pass to the greater king without naming its maker.

He offered the keeper a share of the bronze to be taken from Troy. The old man moved the fragment back across the anvil. No bronze from an untaken city could replace what had already been carried away. He reached beneath his stool and brought out an empty wooden stand. Its shallow curve had been cut to receive the basin's foot; dust lay thick in the hollow.

Odysseus drew off his seal ring. The stone had warmed against his hand, and for a moment its impression remained upon his skin. With that seal his stewards released grain and his shipmasters accepted orders brought by another man. He laid it upon the empty stand.

The keeper closed his fingers around it. Before the moon returned to its present shape, the basin must stand here again. Until then, the ring would remain beneath the altar. Odysseus accepted the term without calling his oarsmen in to witness it. Thestor stood near enough to see the ring disappear into the keeper's pouch.

Only then did the old man uncover the weapons. Six iron tubes lay upon low wooden frames, each too heavy for a man to carry far at arm's length. Beside them stood twenty-five prepared charges in a fitted chest. The keeper counted them with Thestor, moving one small counter for each from a shallow dish to the anvil stone.

He took one charge for the demonstration. The Greeks waited behind the shoulder of the entrance while he turned a tube toward a fallen block outside. Its report drove birds from the cliff. For several breaths the smoke hid the stone; when the wind cleared it, a pale scar showed across its face. One of the rowers had put his hands over his ears. He lowered them and looked at the keeper's fingers, which shook as he steadied the frame.

Thestor counted the chest again. Twenty-four charges remained. The empty place was left empty.

These were the harbor's defenses, not spoil to be divided among the Greeks. Odysseus received them upon that understanding. The keeper kept the ring. The empty basin stand remained before his stool.

The men cut carrying slings from a spare length of sail. Three times on the descent they put the tubes down and went back for the chest. At the narrow ledge, Odysseus went below the burden and braced it against his shoulder while Thestor paid out the cloth from above. A torn edge began to run through the weave. The smith knotted the sling shorter, leaving his own tools for the next journey down.

The tide was returning when they reached the skiff. The oarsmen shifted the chest until the little hull sat level; the weapons took the places where passengers would have put their feet. Thestor fetched his tools last and wedged them under a thwart. Odysseus put his bare right hand against the stern and pushed with the others until the water lifted the keel.

At the entrance above them, the keeper stood with one hand upon the rock. Odysseus settled to an oar. The groove of its loom pressed across the pale band where the ring had been.

* * *

At midday the red prow of Agamemnon's flagship overhung the shingle. Beneath it, six iron tubes rested upon a cedar rack. The king laid his token on the crosspiece, where every lord approaching the trial could see it. He had already assigned places for the weapons among the contingents, as he might allot captured tripods or horses.

Achilles stood apart from the rack. The archers in his own company drew their bows within reach of an answering arrow; even a man who killed at a distance had to expose his body to do it. Here the king remained behind the spectators while Thestor knelt beside the iron. The smith would bear whatever came back from it. The token upon the rack would bear the king's name.

Odysseus brought the chest forward. Meles stayed beside his uncle, holding a clean strip of linen and the small counters used at the sanctuary. Food had put some color back into the boy's mouth, but he rested against the rack when he thought the lords were looking elsewhere.

The target was a length of oak, split from a ruined steering post and set beyond the men. At the king's demand, Thestor prepared one tube. Meles drew back. The report struck the hull above them and returned from it; white smoke rolled among the spectators, and two lords stumbled against one another before they could find the open air. When the oak came into view, a great splinter hung from its middle.

The tube had leaped against its frame. Thestor's right palm and wrist were burned. He backed away with the fingers drawn inward, unable to open them. Meles unwound the linen he had brought, but it was too short to cover the whole wrist. Achilles tore a longer strip from the edge of his cloak and gave it to the boy.

Agamemnon moved toward the chest for another proof. Thestor withdrew his good hand. He would prepare no second discharge while the men handling the iron had neither protection nor a place assured for their dependents aboard the homeward ships. Through Odysseus, he required that the promised passage for Meles be entered upon the fleet's tally before the weapons went forward.

The king kept his hand on the chest. Beyond the ditch, Trojan chariots were moving along the road, with footmen coming down between them. Men on the wall called for the shields to be brought up. Agamemnon looked from the advancing line to the smith's bandaged wrist. Another man could lift a tube, but none of the lords had yet agreed to stand beside one when it fired.

Achilles sent for the shield-men who had waited behind his ships. He placed them around Thestor and Meles, then turned two toward the king's attendants, who had begun to reach for the frames. The operators would go forward inside the Myrmidon shields. Their guards would stay with them, even if the rest of the line advanced.

Those men had held the foremost place in his attacks. Now he was putting them behind the ditch, around a chest and a smith. The lords watched while he counted them out. Agamemnon left his token on the rack, but ordered the passage recorded and let the Myrmidons take their stations.

Thestor returned to the chest. One charge had gone soft beneath its wrapping during the journey from Lekton. He drew a black stroke across it and put it apart from the dry packets. An attendant reached for the spoiled charge before the smith could carry it away; the king would have every part of the sanctuary's loan accounted for. It was laid in a separate fold at the end of the chest. The mark remained visible.

Twenty-three charges were still in their possession: twenty-two dry, one suspect. No second shot had been fired.

Meles closed the chest. Thestor tried to knot the carrying sling with his left hand, and the boy finished the knot for him. The Myrmidons lifted the frames. When they moved toward the ditch, Agamemnon's token swung from the rack, and the men beside it came from Achilles's ships.

* * *

Rain came from the sea before the Trojans reached the ditch. It flattened the grass along the bank and ran down the faces of the men waiting below. Achilles stood above the operators with his shield turned against the slope. Thestor crouched beside a frame, keeping his bandaged wrist beneath his cloak. Meles held the chest off the wet ground upon two stones.

Through the rain Achilles could distinguish Hector's crest. The Trojan prince was advancing on foot beside his chariot, with men following the tracks its wheels had cut. Achilles shifted his spear into his throwing hand. There was room to meet him beyond the ditch if the Greeks moved now.

The first tube fired before he gave that movement. Thestor had turned it toward the crest, but Hector stepped down from a rut as the smoke broke out. The shot struck a shield beyond him and drove its bearer backward. His companions caught him under the arms. A strip of blue wool hung from his wrist, the color worn by the dyers along the lower river; it dragged through the mud as they carried him away.

The line behind Hector checked. The noise had come before any flight they could follow with their eyes. Then another tube fired. Its shot tore into the earth, throwing gravel against the legs of the men nearest it. A third struck among the shields. The Greeks began to climb from the ditch while smoke spread across its mouth.

Hector drew his men farther apart and sent javelin-men along the bank, where the smoke showed him the weapons stood. Achilles saw the change and called his guards back to the operators. A shaft struck the side of the chest. Meles flinched against it, and one of the stones beneath it rolled away.

The packets spilled into the rain.

The boy gathered them against his tunic. All the cloth was wet now; the black stroke upon the spoiled charge had run into the folds. Thestor could not close his burned hand around the burden. He took what Meles brought him and hurried to use it while the last of the smoke still covered the frame.

The fourth charge hissed. No shot crossed the ditch.

Thestor waited, bent low beside the iron. A javelin entered the mud where his shoulder had been. One of the guards seized the frame to pull it under the bank, and Thestor caught the opposite side with his good hand. The near support sank into the softened clay. The tube tipped, struck a stone, and broke its collar with a short, clear sound beneath the shouting. The weight of the frame came down across Meles's leg.

Achilles had reached the lip of the ditch. Hector was within a cast of the spear. He drew his arm back and heard the boy below him.

He turned the shield first. A javelin hit its bronze as he dropped into the trench. He drove its rim into the clay and thrust over it, forcing the foremost Trojan away from the broken frame. The man behind came on before his companion had cleared the spear. Achilles struck him with the shield and gained enough room for one of his own men to kneel beside the boy.

Thestor pulled at the timber. His right hand opened inside the bandage and left a dark patch upon it, but he could not keep his grip. The tube lay across the brace that held Meles down. Beyond the shield the Trojans were gathering for another rush.

The smith took the knife from his belt with his left hand. He cut the sling still binding the iron to the frame. The freed tube rolled against the broken support, then plunged muzzle-first into the water at the bottom of the ditch. The Myrmidon lifted the brace. Thestor reached underneath and drew the boy toward him, leaving the shattered collar and the fine straps of the sanctuary's harness in the clay.

Achilles backed with them. He could no longer see Hector through the smoke, but he heard the change in the Trojan movement: the men at the bank were being called away in groups. Hector had brought his wounded behind the chariots. With the Greek shields now closing upon the ditch, he would not feed more men down the narrow slope to reach a weapon that had ceased firing. His rearward files faced the Greeks while the others withdrew toward the oak.

The Myrmidons did not follow. They carried Meles between two shields until they reached the shelter of the bank. Thestor came behind them, holding the rescued packets against his breast. Rain washed the powder stains from his clothes and left the burn beneath the linen untouched.

Five tubes were brought back upright. The sixth lay with its collar broken under the timber. Nineteen charges remained. Thestor counted them once; Odysseus counted them again before the chest was closed. Three shots and the failed charge had taken four from the twenty-three carried into the ditch.

The Greeks held the approach. Men went out for the wounded under the cover of the shields, and the javelins thrown after Hector fell short of his withdrawing line. Achilles stayed until the last operator had crossed the bank. Only then did he lower his shield.

At the ships, the healer cut the wet cloth from Meles's leg. He could save the boy, but not the foot. Thestor sat beside the bedding with his burned hand laid open upon his knee. He fetched the bundle Meles had packed for the homeward ship. When the healer needed cloth, Thestor took out the unworn tunic and gave it over, leaving the little rasps and the second garment upon the blanket.

Odysseus carried the count to Agamemnon himself. He had no seal to send with another man. Behind him the guards brought five tubes, their frames streaked with the ditch mud, and the closed chest holding nineteen charges.

The king's attendant was pouring water into a broad bronze basin before the meal. Beneath one handle, where the wet metal caught the light, lay the crossed tools and the three broken strokes. Odysseus set down the tally of the weapons. He waited for the attendant to lift the empty jug, then put his unringed hand through the basin's handle.
